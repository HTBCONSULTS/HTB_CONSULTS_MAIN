An idea may be exciting, commercially promising and technically achievable. Yet none of those qualities guarantees that it should become a software product. The real question is whether a clearly defined group of people has a problem worth solving—and whether your proposed solution can solve it sustainably.

I have seen how quickly conversations can move from “I have an idea” to discussions about websites, mobile apps, features and development budgets. My approach at HTB Consult starts earlier: understand the business case, test assumptions and establish what the first working product truly needs to do.

A minimum viable product (MVP) is not a rushed or incomplete version of your ambition. It is the smallest reliable, usable product that allows you to test a defined value proposition with real users and learn what to improve next.

## 1. Start with the problem, not the app

Before asking a developer for a quote, explain the problem without mentioning technology. Who experiences it? When does it occur? What does it cost in time, money, effort or missed opportunity? How is it solved today?

Consider a founder planning an online marketplace for skilled tradespeople. The obvious idea is an app for finding providers. But customer research might reveal that people are less concerned about search and more concerned about trust, availability and accountability. Those discoveries should shape the product before a single screen is designed.

- Identify one primary customer group and its most important unmet need.
- Write a clear problem statement and the outcome customers are seeking.
- Separate evidence from assumptions that still need testing.

> **Founder’s takeaway:** Do not invest heavily in a solution until you understand the problem it is meant to solve.

## 2. Validate demand before committing to development

Validation is not asking friends whether your idea sounds good. It involves observing behaviour and testing whether prospective customers will take meaningful action. Interview potential users, examine alternatives and run a small experiment that tests your most important assumption.

Depending on the idea, a landing page, a clickable prototype, a controlled pilot or a manually delivered service may reveal more than months of development. The goal is not to prove yourself right; it is to learn early enough to make a better decision.

- **Desirability:** Do prospective users care enough to try or pay for the solution?
- **Feasibility:** Can the first version be delivered reliably with available capabilities?
- **Viability:** Can the business model support the cost of acquiring and serving customers?

> **Founder’s takeaway:** Evidence of user behaviour is more useful than enthusiasm alone.

## 3. Define the business model and MVP outcome

A working MVP needs a business objective, not merely a list of screens. Identify the customer action that would demonstrate value: booking a service, completing a purchase, submitting a request or managing an important workflow successfully.

Define the target user, core journey, proposed revenue model, operational responsibilities and success measures. For the tradesperson marketplace, the primary journey might be: customer submits a job request → suitable provider responds → customer confirms → job status is tracked. Features outside that journey may wait until the core proposition is proven.

The MVP must also account for necessary safeguards. Data protection, accessibility, appropriate payment handling and security are not optional extras to be postponed simply because the product is labelled “minimum”.

> **Founder’s takeaway:** Minimum means focused scope—not minimal quality, safety or trust.

## 4. Translate the idea into clear requirements

Many expensive development problems begin with vague instructions such as “build something like this app”. Business analysis converts those ambitions into a shared description of what users need, how the service operates and what successful delivery looks like.

At HTB Consult, this is where discovery, stakeholder engagement, process mapping, business requirements and user journeys become valuable. The outputs do not need to be unnecessarily complicated. They do need to be sufficiently clear for a product team to estimate, design, build and test.

- Document the principal user roles and the tasks each needs to complete.
- Map the current and proposed operating processes, including work done outside the software.
- Prioritise requirements into essential, later and out-of-scope features.
- Define acceptance criteria for each critical feature, including errors and exceptions.

For example, “users can book a service” is not enough. The team needs to know whether availability is real-time, when a booking is confirmed, what happens if payment fails and who resolves a dispute.

> **Founder’s takeaway:** Clarity before development is almost always cheaper than repeated rework during development.

## 5. Design, build and test one complete journey

Start with a simple prototype to confirm that users understand the workflow. Then develop a focused, end-to-end version that works across the frontend, backend and operational processes. Resist the urge to add impressive-looking features that do not contribute to the test you are trying to run.

A practical phased delivery approach is:

- **Design:** Prototype the essential journey and gather feedback.
- **Build:** Implement the minimum reliable feature set and required integrations.
- **Test:** Check functionality, usability, security and important failure scenarios.
- **Pilot:** Release to a defined group of real users and support them closely.
- **Improve:** Fix friction and prioritise changes using actual usage evidence.

Before launch, assign ownership of the domain, source code, hosting, customer data and administrative accounts. Confirm how the product will be supported after the initial build. A successful deployment is not the same thing as a sustainable operating service.

> **Founder’s takeaway:** A working MVP must complete the promised customer journey—not merely look finished in a demo.

## 6. Measure learning and decide what happens next

Your first launch is an experiment, not a declaration that every assumption has been validated. Select a small number of measures linked directly to your objective. These might include completed registrations, successful bookings, activation, conversion, repeat usage, customer feedback, fulfilment time or the cost of serving users.

Examine both outcomes and reasons. If visitors sign up but never complete a booking, the challenge could be trust, availability, pricing, confusing design or operational breakdown—not necessarily a shortage of features.

Use the evidence to decide whether to improve the core journey, change the business model, target a different audience or scale. Effective product management means being willing to revise an original idea when user behaviour tells a different story.

> **Founder’s takeaway:** The value of an MVP lies in the decisions it helps you make.

### A Practical MVP Readiness Checklist

| Area | What to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | We can describe the customer problem clearly. |
| Evidence | We have tested important assumptions with prospective users. |
| Business case | We understand the customer value and basic commercial logic. |
| Scope | We know which capabilities are essential for the first release. |
| Requirements | Critical journeys and acceptance criteria are documented. |
| Delivery | We have a realistic plan, ownership and support arrangements. |
| Measurement | We know how we will judge whether the pilot is working. |

## Final Thought: Build to Learn, Then Build to Grow

The strongest founders are not necessarily those who launch the most features or spend the largest budgets. They are those who ask better questions, take informed decisions and turn feedback into progress.

Before you build your next platform, ask: **What is the smallest dependable solution that could demonstrate real value to the people I want to serve?**

If that answer is clear, you are much closer to building a working MVP—and a business worth growing.
